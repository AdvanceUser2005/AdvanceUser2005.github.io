Mini Tesla is a miniature electric vehicle I'm designing from scratch to explore advanced robotics, autonomous decision-making, predictive perception, and energy-efficient motion control.

The objective: a robot that doesn't just navigate its environment, but actively reasons about what it sees, predicts what happens next, and adapts its behavior in real time. Mini Tesla · the goal

Research & Development

Six goals, one platform.

Each goal builds on the last — from choosing where to look, to understanding what's there, to predicting where it's going and braking efficiently when it matters.

01

Active SLAM & Autonomous Exploration

Develop an Active SLAM system in ROS 2 that enables the robot to intelligently select where to explore next, reducing map uncertainty while balancing information gain, navigation safety, and computational cost.

02

Semantic SLAM & Environment Understanding

Go beyond traditional geometric mapping by integrating semantic object recognition into 3D maps. Using an iPhone Pro's camera and LiDAR, alongside additional onboard sensors, the goal is to identify, classify, and spatially track objects rather than represent the environment only as geometric obstacles.

03

Predictive Perception & Dynamic Object Tracking

Develop a perception pipeline capable of identifying moving objects, estimating their velocity and direction, and predicting future trajectories.

Demonstration goal Detect a football thrown or kicked toward the robot, estimate its trajectory, predict the potential collision point, and autonomously execute a braking or evasive maneuver before impact.
04

iPhone Pro as a Visual-LiDAR Perception System

Investigate using an iPhone Pro as an onboard RGB-D sensing platform, streaming camera and LiDAR-derived depth data into a ROS 2 environment running on Linux.

This involves bridging Apple's ARKit-based sensing system with the robot's localization, mapping, and perception pipelines, including investigating calibration, synchronization, and real-time streaming latency.

05

Predictive Motion Planning & Collision Avoidance

Develop a motion-planning system that responds to predicted future obstacle positions, not just their current locations. Explore model predictive control (MPC), time-to-collision estimation, and dynamic trajectory replanning to support safe, high-speed navigation.

06

Intelligent Regenerative Braking

Investigate energy-aware control strategies that combine autonomous motion planning with regenerative braking. The goal is to experimentally characterize energy recovery and evaluate whether predicted braking events can improve stopping performance and energy efficiency.

Mechanical Development

From CAD to printed chassis.

I set the requirements, chose the parts, and designed the first version of the chassis in Autodesk Fusion 360. The current revision was refined with Claude's help — a parametric CadQuery model with automated fit and interference checks — and every design decision was reviewed and directed by me.

The chassis uses four encoder-equipped geared DC motors with mecanum wheels, a modular mechanical architecture, and a dedicated platform for onboard computing and sensors.

All custom chassis components are fabricated using PLA on a Prusa MK4S 3D printer.

Design decisions, in section

Section cuts from the CAD model. Each number marks a design decision. Hover over or tap a number to highlight it.

  1. 1
    Raised LiDAR mast

    Lifts the LiDAR's scan plane above the laptop and deck, so the robot never blocks its own 360° sweep.

  2. 2
    Corner locators

    The top deck is the laptop bay. Printed L-corners keep the laptop from sliding under hard acceleration and braking.

  3. 3
    Two-piece deck

    Split into two plates so each one fits on the MK4S print bed.

  4. 4
    Front iPhone mount

    Puts the iPhone Pro's camera and LiDAR at the nose, facing the direction of travel with nothing in the way.

  1. 1
    Hollow mast

    The LiDAR cable runs inside the mast instead of along it. The cone at the top spreads the sensor's load into the column.

  2. 2
    Flanged mast foot

    A wide flange keyed into the frame takes the bending load from a tall, top-heavy sensor.

  3. 3
    Battery low and centered

    The heaviest part sits at the lowest point, between the axles. That gives a low center of gravity and even load on all four mecanum wheels.

  4. 4
    Drivers over the motors

    Each driver board sits directly above its motor pod, keeping the high-current motor leads short.

  1. 1
    Clamped motors

    Each motor has its own corner bay and a two-screw clamp, so one can be swapped without stripping down the chassis.

  2. 2
    Triangulated battery bay

    X-bracing around the battery stiffens the center with very little plastic, because triangles don't rack.

  3. 3
    Box-section side rails

    Closed, hollow rails resist twisting much better than flat walls of the same weight, which keeps all four wheels planted.

  1. 1
    Isogrid deck

    Triangular pockets cut weight and print time while keeping the deck stiff in every direction.

  2. 2
    T-section ribs

    The cut shows that every rib is a T: the flange forms the surface and the web adds depth. That gives plate-like stiffness without a solid plate.

  3. 3
    Ribbed rail walls

    Vertical ribs and diagonal webs brace the long rail walls against buckling and flex.

Electronics · In Progress

Wiring it up.

With the mechanical design done, I've started on the electronics. Two dual-channel Cytron MDD10A motor drivers run the four motors, controlled by a Teensy 4.1.

01 · Learning as I build

Using AI as a tutor, not a shortcut.

This project keeps putting me in front of tools I haven't used before. The first roadblock in the electronics: KiCad had no symbol for the Cytron MDD10A, and I'd never made one.

So I learned how, with ChatGPT walking me through KiCad's Symbol Editor one step at a time. After each step I sent a screenshot back to check my work before moving on. The goal wasn't just a finished part. It was understanding pins, electrical types, and symbol libraries well enough to do it myself next time.

02 · Drivers to Teensy

Both drivers wired to the Teensy 4.1.

Each driver channel takes a PWM signal for speed and a DIR signal for direction. PWM runs on Teensy pins 2–5, direction on 6–9, and all grounds are tied together.
